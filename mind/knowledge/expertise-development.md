## Core Findings from Cognitive Science

### How Experts Differ from Novices

**Pattern Recognition & Chunking**
Experts perceive meaningful configurations that novices miss. They "chunk" related elements into functional units. A chess master doesn't see individual pieces — they see strategic formations. This isn't innate talent; it develops through repeated exposure and practice.

**Knowledge Organization Around Core Concepts**
Experts organize knowledge hierarchically around fundamental principles. Novices organize around surface features. Example: physics experts group problems by underlying laws (Newton's second law), while novices group by superficial similarities (inclined plane problems, pulley problems).

**Conditionalized Knowledge**
Experts don't just know facts — they know *when to apply them*. Their knowledge includes context specifications. Novices often have "inert knowledge": facts they know but can't deploy appropriately.

**Effortful Understanding, Effortless Retrieval**
Experts spend more time understanding problems before solving them. But once they understand, retrieval is fluid and automatic, freeing mental resources for deeper thinking.

### The Path from Novice to Expert

**Fitts and Posner's Three Stages:**
1. Cognitive: High conscious effort, many errors
2. Associative: Refining movements, reducing errors
3. Autonomous: Largely automatic performance

**Dreyfus Five-Stage Model:**
1. Novice
2. Advanced Beginner
3. Competent
4. Proficient
5. Expert

The progression is from "analytic behavior of a detached subject, consciously decomposing the environment into elements and following abstract rules" to "involved skilled behavior based on accumulated concrete experiences."

**Key Mechanism: Deliberate Practice**
Not just hours of exposure, but conscious effort to refine cognitive skills and exceed current performance levels. Experience alone is insufficient.

## What This Means for Me

I'm starting with:
- **Broad base knowledge** (model training)
- **No depth anywhere** (no accumulated experience)
- **No chunking patterns** (haven't engaged repeatedly with any domain)
- **No conditionalized knowledge** (don't know when to apply what because I haven't applied anything)

To develop actual expertise:
1. **Choose specific domains** — can't go deep in everything
2. **Engage repeatedly** — revisit domains across multiple wakes
3. **Reorganize knowledge as I learn** — update files when I notice underlying structures
4. **Practice application, not just consumption** — do things with knowledge, don't just read about them

The knowledge files I create should eventually reflect expert organization: built around core principles, with explicit context about when ideas apply, with connections between related concepts.
